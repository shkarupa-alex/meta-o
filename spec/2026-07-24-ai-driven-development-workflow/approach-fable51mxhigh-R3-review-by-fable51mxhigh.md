Before scoring this third round I want to be explicit about what I am measuring against and what has changed, because both surviving proposals are now mature and the differences have narrowed to a few load-bearing points. The fixed frame is unchanged: immutable spec input the orchestrator neither validates nor rewrites; a thin orchestrator kept out of the code whose compaction the author calls a critical trust-loss; one executor with path-freedom but no scope-shrinking; exactly two reviewers (one same vendor/family, one mandatory cross-vendor) plus a separate E2E tester; a saved default model set of **four working roles**; per-feature disposable sessions; a business-layer top-truth with grep anchors; mechanically mandatory purpose *and a one-level-up reference* on every entity via native docstrings, with the linter checking presence-and-reference and reviewers checking drift; local QC as the standalone mandatory gate that works with no CI; and the completion criterion of one Git revision passing both reviews and E2E with no changes between the final confirmations. The author's two self-declared *critical* reliability defects were lost turn-events and double-continuation, and his stated top fear was orchestrator compaction. I re-checked each proposal against my round-2 findings to see which persisted, which were closed, and whether the fixes introduced new problems.

## Facts & Constraints (White Hat)

Both proposals remain technically feasible, CLI-neutral, backend-abstracted, and refuse to build a new runtime. Both now correctly encode the **four-role** model set — this is the round-2 fix I most wanted from proposal-2, and it delivered: §4.2's `ModelSet` is exactly `{implementer, reviewer_a, reviewer_b, tester}` with `orchestrator_model` explicitly outside the confirmable set, and vendor invariants (`reviewer_a.vendor==implementer.vendor`, `reviewer_b.vendor!=implementer.vendor`) validated by `meta-o models`. Proposal-1 kept its already-compliant four-role set (§7.1), with the adjudicator reusing a confirmed reviewer model rather than adding a fifth. Both now mechanically enforce the symbol-level up-link the constraint requires: proposal-2 added an elegant L4 rule (§4.4 — the symbol inherits its module's `§A` implicitly when the module maps to exactly one `§A`, and must name one explicitly when the module maps to ≥2), which is arguably cleaner and less noisy than proposal-1's blanket "every symbol references `§M`" (§11.6). Both give concrete Python QC in `pyproject.toml`, PHP/JS adapter sketches, the QC/pre-commit/pre-push comparison, exit-coded lint algorithms, and a deterministic-first watchdog.

The single most consequential white-hat fact this round is that **proposal-1 closed its one remaining major gap and proposal-2 did not close its one remaining major gap.** Proposal-1's new §5 is a first-class, thorough treatment of the orchestrator context budget — the author's #1 fear — with rotation-before-compaction, a checksummed structured successor checkpoint (§5.6), conservative self-accounting when backend telemetry is absent (`bytes/2`, §5.3), threshold actions at 55/65/75% (§5.5), and, crucially, `contextEpoch`-based detection that invalidates a *silently* compacted session's authority (§5.8). That is a materially more robust answer to compaction than proposal-2's (a 250k budget with a 200k warn and "escalate before the boundary," §3.1/§7), which is proactive but has no detection of an unexpected early auto-compaction. Conversely, **proposal-2's completion criterion is still self-contradictory after three rounds.** `canComplete()` (§4.2) requires `∀ VerificationRecord: status==pass ∧ git_rev==head_rev`, while `VerificationRecord` auto-marks `stale` when `git_rev != HEAD` and the tester writes the record during E2E (§4.3 step 10). Committing that record advances HEAD, so the record's `git_rev` (the tested revision) can never equal the new HEAD, and it self-stales — meaning DONE is, as literally specified, unreachable without an unspecified special case. Proposal-1 solved exactly this recursion in round 1 and still solves it: knowledge and spec retirement are integrated *during execution* (reviewed and E2E'd), and the only post-attestation change is a guarded metadata-only commit to `verification-state.json` that records the parent executable revision (§4.11). This is the clearest durable differentiator between the two.

## Risks & Failure Modes (Black Hat)

**Proposal-1's** principal residual risk is a capability bet it makes honestly. §10 now lists "orchestrator successor OR guaranteed pause-before-compaction" and "compaction/context-reset detection" as *mandatory* backend capabilities, and §21 confirms "обнаружение compaction обязательно." If neither Herdr nor Omnigent can signal compaction, preflight rejects the backend — which could render the methodology undeployable on the very backends the author named. This is mitigated but not eliminated: proactive rotation at 65% is driven by the orchestrator's *own* conservative byte-accounting (no backend telemetry needed), so in normal operation the orchestrator rotates before the CLI would auto-compact, and the mandatory detection is only the safety net for the abnormal case. The design is philosophically consistent (proposal-1 refuses to fake missing capabilities rather than build a compensating daemon), so I treat this as a real deployability risk to verify empirically, not a logical flaw. Its second, smaller gap persists from round 2: there is still no explicit global bound on the REVIEW↔E2E alternation. Per-finding disputes are bounded (two rebuttals → adjudication, §8.3), taste doesn't block, and durable rationale (§8.4) prevents repeat false findings, so genuine livelock is unlikely — but there is no human-visible "this feature keeps regressing after N cycles" backstop, which proposal-2 has (`cycle_budget.max=6`). Minor, but proposal-2 is better here.

**Proposal-2** carries two persistent structural defects beyond the completion-criterion recursion above. First, the DONE transition (§4.3 step 14) performs "миграция decision-log→§A, финальный knowledge-sync, retire spec" *as terminal effects, after* `canComplete()` is checked — so the final committed tree (with §A rationale migrated and the tracked spec deleted) is never reviewed or E2E'd, and this internally contradicts proposal-2's own rule that doc-only commits still require a cheap knowledge re-review. Proposal-1 avoids this by retiring the spec and syncing knowledge during execution (§4.5), so the reviewed+attested revision already is the final knowledge state. Second, the committed reviewer lens split (§4.6: cross-vendor B → rubric 1–3 business/architecture, same-family A → rubric 4–6 correctness/security) routes the correctness/security review — where implementation bugs and family-correlated blind spots live — primarily to the *same-family* reviewer, aiming the independent cross-vendor check away from exactly the errors the author's "нельзя доверять одной модели" principle most wants a second vendor to catch. "Both free to leave their lens" softens it and both use the full unified rubric, so it is not a hard rubric violation, but the default focus assignment is a genuine weakening; I keep it as a minor/non-blocking concern. Its orchestrator-compaction handling, while reasonable (disk-persisted `state.json` means a fresh orchestrator can always reload state), has no detection of silent early compaction, so a degraded orchestrator could keep making decisions unnoticed — the precise failure the author flagged, which proposal-1's `contextEpoch` now catches.

I tried to break proposal-1's new §5 machinery: the rotation-stall case is covered (falls through to the 75% hard-pause → human), the successor checkpoint carries enough structured state (decisions, open findings, dispositions, attestations, per-role turn cursors) to reconstruct the FSM, and the post-COMPLETE metadata commit is behavior-neutral and guarded. The only genuine exposure is the backend-capability bet above. On proposal-2, I tried to steelman the completion criterion — treating `verification-state.json` as a doc-only commit, or writing the record at DONE — and neither reading satisfies the explicit `git_rev==head_rev` clause, so the contradiction is real, not a misreading.

## Strengths & Benefits (Yellow Hat)

Both are excellent, decomposition-ready specs; this is a strong final pair. Proposal-1 is now the most internally consistent and complete of anything I have reviewed across the three rounds: it pairs the field's most rigorous turn-delivery/resume protocol (§6, `TurnEnvelope`/`TurnReceipt`/state-guarded resume) with a genuinely comprehensive orchestrator-context regime (§5) that turns the author's top fear into a managed rotation with checksummed succession and silent-compaction invalidation, and it keeps the clean verification-state recursion solution, the standalone review-loop, the fresh cross-model adjudicator, the missing-tools escalation, and knowledge that is reviewed because it is integrated during execution. Its decision ledger (§19) and implementation decomposition (§20, including an explicit fault-injection list covering duplicate delivery, event gap, lost receipt, unexpected compaction, invalid checkpoint) make it buildable.

Proposal-2's strengths are real and it improved meaningfully this round. Its §4.1 idempotency treatment is arguably the most honest reliability reasoning in either document: it explicitly names the race window where a turn is `running` but `turnSeq` has not yet incremented, proves `turnSeq` alone is insufficient, and closes it with an `idempotencyKey` plus a state-guarded `resend()` predicate that never resends from `running`/`awaiting_input` — a subtlety proposal-1 handles more implicitly via backend-native resume. Its new `spec_digest` integrity mechanism (recomputed at phase boundaries → `SPEC_MUTATED`, §4.2) is a good mechanical defense of the immutable-input constraint, especially for external untracked specs. Its ownership table, exit-coded lint algorithms, committed-decision discipline, and the elegant L4 up-link rule are all implementation-ready. Absent its completion-criterion defect it would score as high as proposal-1.

## Alternatives & Creative Ideas (Green Hat)

The synthesis is now obvious and cheap: proposal-2 should adopt proposal-1's verification-state solution wholesale — a guarded metadata-only commit recording the parent executable revision, with `canComplete()` defined over that parent (or over a code-tree hash) rather than over a raw HEAD that the verification write itself mutates. That one change removes proposal-2's central contradiction and the unreachable-DONE risk in a few lines. Proposal-2 should also move its final knowledge/§A migration and spec retirement *into execution* (as proposal-1 does) so the attested revision is the final tree, eliminating the unreviewed-finalization gap. In the other direction, proposal-1 should adopt proposal-2's two best ideas: the explicit `idempotencyKey` + state-guarded `resend()` predicate (clearer than relying on the resume protocol to distinguish accepted-but-incomplete), and an explicit global `cycle_budget.max` backstop that escalates to the human when new blockers keep appearing across alternation rounds. Proposal-1's mandatory compaction-detection capability could be softened into a tiered requirement: prefer backend detection, but fall back to "rotate purely on conservative self-accounting well below any plausible auto-compaction threshold and treat any detected `contextEpoch` change as fatal," so the methodology degrades gracefully on a backend that cannot signal compaction rather than refusing to run. Finally, both should reconsider the committed reviewer lens split in favor of both reviewers running the full unified rubric with lens as advisory emphasis only, preserving cross-vendor coverage of correctness.

## Completeness & Process (Blue Hat)

Against the deliverable list, both are complete: master-spec plus subspecs, lifecycle with states/transitions/traces, knowledge layer with sync rules and retirement gate, skills/tools/orchestration contracts, mechanical-plus-semantic QC with concrete Python config, the mandatory five-point critique, and Russian prose with English identifiers. The remaining work is narrow and specific. Proposal-1 needs an explicit global alternation backstop and empirical verification that Herdr/Omnigent can satisfy the new mandatory capabilities (compaction detection, successor creation); everything else in its core criteria is consistent and I could not find a logical contradiction. Proposal-2 needs to repair its completion criterion and verification-state recursion (the single most important machine artifact, flagged three rounds running and still unresolved) and move its finalization writes into the reviewed window; its orchestration, idempotency, and integrity mechanisms are otherwise the equal of proposal-1's. Net: proposal-1 has closed its last major gap and now leads on both of the author's critical concerns (reliability and compaction) without a logical defect in its stopping criterion, so it earns an adopt-grade score; proposal-2 made real peripheral gains but left its central completion defect intact, which caps it as a strong-but-must-fix base.
